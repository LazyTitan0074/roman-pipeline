## Chapter 1: Cinderella

### Scene 1: The Servants' Wing

The servants' wing smelled of lye and damp stone, and Leah Estia had long since stopped noticing. She knelt on the cold flagstones with a scrubbing brush in her raw hands, working at a stain that had been there since before she was born, probably. She was twenty years old, a head shorter than the guards at the door, her long chestnut hair pinned up simply because loose hair got in the way of work. Nobody had called her beautiful in years. Nobody called her anything except when they wanted something.

A shadow fell across her bucket.

"Still at it?" Cassian stood over her with his hands clasped behind his back, fourteen years old and already an expert in the only art his mother had ever taught him: making other people feel small. "Mother says the kitchens need a proper scour today. Last night's boar is still on the walls."

"Good morning, Cassian," Leah said.

He nudged her bucket with the toe of his boot — not hard, just enough to ripple the grey water. He never touched her. He didn't need to. "She'll be checking. Don't leave the corners."

"I'm still working on this corridor."

"A queen can manage two tasks, surely." He smiled the way Mirela smiled, like a door closing. "Unless Father's daughter isn't up to it."

Leah's grip tightened on the brush. "I'm sorry. Did I do something wrong?"

"Wrong? No. You're doing exactly what you're told." He turned on his heel and walked away down the clean half of the floor she had already finished, boots clicking, leaving her the dirty half and the kitchens besides.

She stayed kneeling a moment longer, breathing through her nose, then picked up the brush again. Eight years of this. Eight years since the rooms had shrunk and the servants had stopped meeting her eyes and the boy with her father's chin had learned to call her by her surname, like a servant, like a nobody. Estia. As if the name were the problem and not the woman who had given the order.

### Scene 2: The Betrothal Contract

The summons came at midday, written in a clerk's hand: the queen's office, at once.

Leah smoothed her apron — a useless gesture; nothing smoothed this dress — and went up the stairs she had not climbed as a resident in two years. The queen's office smelled of Mirela's heavy perfume and old parchment. Mirela sat behind the oak desk the way she sat behind everything in Valdris now: entirely. She was past forty and looked it the way marble looks old — harder, not softer. Beside her stood a man Leah had seen twice in her life and hoped never to see again: Lord Varnholt of the northern passes, sixty-two years old, with money, soldiers, and the patience of someone who has already waited out two wives.

"You wished to see me, Your Majesty?" Leah said from the doorway.

"Come closer. Don't hover like a scullery maid."

Leah came closer and fixed her eyes on the griffon carved into the desk.

Mirela unrolled a parchment with a snap. "A betrothal contract. Between you and Lord Varnholt. His lands guard the northern passes. Your union gives Valdris stability, and it gives you —" a pause exactly as long as a held breath "— a future."

Leah looked at the old man's grey stone eyes and then away. "May I ask — why now?"

"Because the kingdom cannot carry an unmarried heiress forever, and because I will not." Mirela leaned forward. "Sign, and you will be a lady of the north. Respected. Secure. Refuse, and the crown's generosity ends. Titles, lands, name — all of it. You will walk out of this castle with the clothes on your back and learn what the Lower City does to girls with no one."

It was not a choice. It was a door with a wall painted on one side.

"May I have time to consider?"

"Until dawn." Mirela smiled. "Rumours of an eastern host grow daily, and I want this settled before fear settles it for us. You may go."

Leah went, with the perfume following her down the corridor like something with legs.

### Scene 3: The Locket

Her room was a box with a cot and a washstand and a window onto a courtyard she was not allowed to enter. Leah sat on the cot and took the small tarnished silver locket from under her pillow, the one thing of her mother's she had managed to keep. She had been twelve when the fever came. Twelve, and the palace had whispered of a sudden sickness, and then the whispering had stopped the way whispering stops when someone important decides it stops.

What she remembered, when she let herself: Mirela, then only a lady-in-waiting, standing too close to the doctors. The cloying sweetness of a particular brew she had brought the queen every evening. Hands trembling around the cup. A smile that never reached the eyes.

It itched at her tonight, the way a wound itches before it opens. Almost a thought. Not quite.

And her father, two years gone on the front — officially a hero's death, songs in the taverns, a statue already green at the edges. After that, the shrinking. The cold. The boy calling her Estia.

She closed her fingers around the locket until the metal hurt her palm. Varnholt's keep in the north. Sixty-two years old and already widowed twice. Girls who married men like that did not come back to the capital with stories. They sent one letter, or none, and the north kept them the way winter keeps things.

She could not sign it. To sign was to walk into a grave with better curtains.

She stood up. Her legs were unsteady, but they held. "Thank you," she whispered — to whom, she didn't know; the habit of gratitude had outlived everyone worth thanking. Then she reached under the cot for the satchel: a few coins, bread going hard at the edges, the locket. It was not enough. It would have to be enough.

She would run.

### Scene 4: Flight Through Shadows

She went through the servants' corridors, the maze she knew better than her own face. Past the kitchens, loud even at this hour. Past the laundry, asleep. Her worn boots were too loud and then, by luck or mercy, not loud enough to matter — a dropped pan somewhere covered ten of her steps, and a drunk guardsman's singing covered ten more.

The postern gate's hinges screamed when she pushed. She froze, counting heartbeats, certain the whole castle had heard. Nothing came. She squeezed through sideways, stone scraping her shoulder, and fell out into the cold clean air of the wrong side of the wall.

The Lower City hit her all at once: smoke, refuse, river damp, frying fat from a stall still open for the night trade. Narrow streets leaning in like listeners. She pulled her hood low and walked, and kept walking, past shuttered shops and taverns leaking light and laughter she had no part in. A dog barked itself hoarse at her from a doorway. A woman laughed too loud somewhere above. Nobody looked at her twice, and that — after eight years of being looked at exactly the wrong way — felt like a hand letting go of her throat.

Twice she heard patrol boots and pressed herself into doorways that smelled of piss and old rain. Twice they passed. Her stomach was a knot. Her feet were already blistered. Behind her, on its hill, the castle sat dark against a sky going grey at the edges, and dawn — Mirela's deadline — was coming whether she signed or not.

She did not look back again. She watched the gutters instead, and the signs, and asked a toothless old man the way downhill with coins she could barely spare, and went downhill, toward the river smell and the noise, because noise meant people and people meant somewhere to disappear.

### Scene 5: The Red Velvet Inn

She found it by the light. Every other door on the street was shut and dark; this one spilled lamplight and fiddle music onto the cobbles, and above it hung a sign painted the color of dried blood: a slipper, a rose. The Red Velvet Inn. Even Leah, who knew nothing of the Lower City, had heard the kitchens whisper the name. The costliest brothel in the capital. Where officers and merchants' sons spent fortunes, and where — the whispers said, the important part — information was worth more than anything bought upstairs.

She pushed the door open because there was nowhere else to push.

Inside: heat, perfume, wine, bodies. A fiddler sawing away in a corner. Laughter like breaking glass. Leah stood dripping street water onto a floor cleaner than any she had scrubbed in years, hood slipping back, too tired to pull it up again — and a woman came toward her through the crowd as if the crowd had been warned.

She was tall, past forty, mahogany hair swept back from a face that missed nothing, a crimson gown that cost more than Leah's entire satchel, satchel's street, satchel's city. Her eyes were amber, bright as a struck match, and they fixed on Leah with no surprise at all.

"Well," the woman said. "The night brings me a stray."

Leah's throat closed. "I'm sorry. May I — I only need somewhere to rest. One night. I can pay—" She stopped, because the woman was smiling, and the smile was not unkind, only calculating, the way a merchant smiles at goods.

"I know what you are, little bird," the woman said quietly. "A princess doesn't wash off in the rain. Sit down before you fall down."

Leah sat. The cushion was velvet. Everything in her ached.

"My father died on the front, two years ago," she heard herself say. It came out all at once, like water through a cracked dam. "My stepmother wants me married to Lord Varnholt. I ran."

"Varnholt." The woman poured wine with her own hand and pushed the cup across. "Sixty-two years old and twice a widower. You were right to run." She studied Leah over the rim of her own cup. "I am Elowen. This house is mine. You are safe here tonight — at a price, which we will discuss when you can think. Drink."

Leah drank. The wine burned warm all the way down.

From a nearby table, a drunk officer was telling the room about riders from the east and a host on the move, numbers too big to believe, and everyone laughed except Elowen, whose amber eyes flicked toward him once, briefly, the way a cat's ears turn toward a sound.

"Thank you," Leah whispered.

"Don't thank me yet, little bird," said Elowen. "Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow we talk about what a runaway princess is worth."
